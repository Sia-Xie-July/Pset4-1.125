Before the Merge is a three-part series for technical leads of 3–10-person software teams deciding how much work to delegate to coding agents.

The first piece in this series separated workflow demonstrations from delivery outcomes. The second separated commercial claims from measured evidence. This final piece is practical. New agent options keep arriving, but none of this month's retained sources measures a small team's delivery outcomes. The defensible next step is therefore a bounded, reversible evaluation of your own team's work before granting an agent more autonomy. The plan below is our proposal, not an experiment we have conducted.

Three sources, three different kinds of evidence

On 13 October, GitHub's changelog announced Gemini 4 Argon in GitHub Copilot. It states that the model is available to Copilot Pro+, Max, Business and Enterprise users and can be selected in the model picker. [03-c1] That is evidence of an available option. Availability does not by itself demonstrate faster delivery, fewer defects or less human review, and we do not treat the announcement as a measurement of those outcomes.

Cloudflare's Birthday Week 2026 roundup, published on 5 October, reports that automated traffic surpassed human activity and describes the influence of agent-driven recommendations on consumer choices. [03-c2] This is a vendor's account of a broader agent ecosystem. It helps explain why teams face more agent-related choices, but the retained passage says nothing about coding productivity. A statement about Internet traffic should not become a claim about a software team's performance.

METR's 14 October post on how it works with AI companies states its goal of giving the public accurate, timely and independent evidence about AI loss-of-control risk, by collecting concrete evidence and publishing what it finds, while noting that many relevant claims remain open scientific questions. [03-c3] METR's subject is frontier-lab risk assessment, not a development team. We borrow only its principle, explicit evidence gathered before a judgment, and keep that difference in scope visible.

From distinctions to a decision process

Together, the three sources separate an available capability, an ecosystem narrative and an evaluation principle. Since none of them measures a small team's delivery, the decision to expand autonomy has to rest on evidence the team produces itself. [03-c4] The right question is not whether a new model is impressive. It is whether a particular use improves an outcome you care about, at an acceptable cost and level of risk.

Step one: choose a bounded task. Pick work with observable acceptance criteria. Avoid starting with a change whose failure would be hard to detect or reverse. Write down what the agent may do, what it may not do, and who remains responsible for approval. Check which models and permissions are actually enabled for your organisation, since vendor defaults can change.

Step two: fix the comparison before you see the result. Use similar recent work as a baseline where possible, and record differences that make the comparison imperfect. Count time spent specifying, reviewing, testing and correcting, not only time until the first code appears. Keep tool usage and cost visible.

Step three: inspect the outcome. Did the change satisfy the intended behaviour? What defects or rework appeared? Did reviewers understand the change well enough to maintain it? A fast first draft that creates substantial later work may not be a delivery improvement.

Step four: decide the rules in advance. Agree beforehand what result would justify expanding, repeating or stopping the pilot. A positive result on one task supports another bounded test, not unrestricted autonomy. A negative result may reveal a poor task fit rather than prove that every agent is ineffective.

What to watch next

Over the next editions we will look for evidence this month lacked: practitioner reports that include review time and rework, comparisons that state a baseline, and accounts from teams where agent adoption did not work. Vendor announcements will keep coming; the useful signal is whether anyone measures what happens after the code is generated.

Limitations

Our evidence cannot supply a universal threshold or guarantee a benefit. Two of the three sources are vendors describing their own products and ecosystem, and the third works on a different problem. A single team's pilot will be small, and task differences and evaluator judgment will affect it. But a transparent pilot makes the next decision inspectable. That is the series takeaway: define the job, examine the claim, and measure the outcome before expanding autonomy.
