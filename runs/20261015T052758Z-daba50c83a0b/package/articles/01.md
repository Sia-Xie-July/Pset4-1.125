# More Agent Output, Better Delivery? What Changed This Month

For the technical lead of a small team, the question this month is not whether a coding agent can produce code. It can. The question is what a team should change when agents start to take part in planning, implementation and verification. Three practitioner sources from the past few weeks point to the same development: agent work is being organised into more explicit structures, with specifications, staged plans, iterative check-ins and clearer job definitions. What none of them shows is that this structure improves delivery. That gap is the subject of this piece.

## A specification-driven workflow

At an AIMUG meetup talk uploaded on 9 October, Jake Cukjati walks through what he calls a coding factory. Around five minutes and forty seconds in, he describes taking a canonical specification into a workflow tool, where agents plan the implementation and then, in his words, “the agents go through and test everything, implement it.” [01-c1]

That is a practitioner describing his own process. It tells us what he does. It does not tell us how long his reviews take, how often the agents' tests miss a problem, or whether fewer defects reach users. Those are separate outcomes, and they need separate evidence. A specification makes intended behaviour visible, and a test checks part of it, but neither guarantees that the specification is complete or that the test asks the right question. Someone on the team still decides what acceptable behaviour means, including the failure cases.

## An iterative loop, not a perfect prompt

In a video published on 7 October, Salvatore Sanfilippo (antirez) pushes back on the idea that success with an agent comes from writing the right prompt. Around three minutes in, he describes instead an ongoing process with the agent: design, writing code, verification and small changes, repeated. [01-c2] He speaks Italian; our evidence is YouTube's automatic English captions, so this is a paraphrase rather than a quotation, and the exact wording is uncertain.

The point matters for a team because it describes agent use as a sequence of human decisions rather than a single handoff. Each loop is a chance to catch a wrong direction early, and each loop also costs attention.

## Defining the agent's job

In a lecture published on 13 October, The Modern SDE sets aside a borrowed list of agent types and groups agents by what they do. [01-c3] We use that distinction narrowly. Before evaluating an agent, define its job. An assistant that suggests a change and an agent that executes a task should not automatically receive the same permissions or be judged by the same standard. That application is our interpretation, not a result from the video.

## What the three add up to

Read together, these accounts point to a shift from prompting to process: define the task, make the intended behaviour explicit, and keep the work inspectable at each step. [01-c4] That is the development worth a team's attention this month. It is also where the evidence stops. None of the three sources measures review time, rework, defect rates or delivery speed. Moving verification into the agent's loop may simply move the work. Someone still writes the specification, reads the test output and judges whether the result is right.

So the honest answer to the title question is: more structure, not yet better delivery. The structure is a hypothesis a team can test.

## A bounded way to test it

A team could try one bounded maintenance task. Before starting, write down the expected behaviour and who will review the change. During the work, keep the agent's plan, proposed code and test results visible. Afterwards, record review time, corrections and unresolved risks, and compare them with a similar task done without the agent. This is a proposed evaluation, not an experiment we conducted.

There is a fair counterargument. Adding checkpoints and specifications costs time, and for small changes the overhead may exceed the benefit. That is exactly why the next decision should be reversible and measured rather than a blanket expansion of autonomy.

## Limitations

All three sources are self-reported practitioner or teaching accounts, without baselines. One relies on machine-translated captions. We have not heard from teams for whom structured agent workflows failed, and nothing here speaks to long-term maintenance cost. More generated code is not the same as better delivery. In the next piece we look at which productivity claims survive scrutiny.
